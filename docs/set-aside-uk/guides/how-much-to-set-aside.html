How much to set aside for tax as a UK sole trader (2026/27)

A short guide to Income Tax and Class 4 National Insurance on trading profit for 6 April 2026 to 5 April 2027, for sole traders in England, Wales and Northern Ireland.

There is no single percentage

How much to set aside depends on profit after allowable expenses, not on turnover. Income Tax is charged on taxable income after the Personal Allowance. Class 4 National Insurance is charged on profits. They use different tests, even when the numbers coincide.

For 2026/27, GOV.UK lists these figures for England, Northern Ireland and Wales:

  • Personal Allowance £12,570. It goes down by £1 for every £2 of income above £100,000, and it can go down to zero.
  • Basic rate 20% on taxable income up to £37,700.
  • Higher rate 40% from £37,701 to £125,140.
  • Additional rate 45% over £125,141.

Class 4 for 2026/27 is 6% on profits over £12,570 up to £50,270, and 2% on profits over £50,270. If profits are £7,105 or more, Class 2 is treated as paid and you do not have to pay it. If profits are under £7,105, voluntary Class 2 is £3.65 a week. The calculator does not add voluntary Class 2 to the pot.

Scotland uses different Income Tax bands. This page does not.

A £30,000 example that matches the calculator

These steps use the same rates as the free calculator. They assume £30,000 is your only income, you are in England, Wales or Northern Ireland, and you have not paid any of this year’s tax outside Self Assessment.

Taxable profit£30,000.00
Personal Allowance£12,570.00
Taxable income£17,430.00
Income Tax at 20%£3,486.00
Class 4 at 6% on profits over £12,570£1,045.80
Estimated 2026/27 bill£4,531.80

That bill is 15.1% of the £30,000 profit. In this example the allowance and the Class 4 lower limit are both £12,570, so both calculations use £17,430. Above £100,000 those tests diverge, because the Personal Allowance tapers and Class 4 does not.

Dividing the bill by 12 is £377.65 a month. That is a planning aid, not a required payment. It is not enough for 31 January 2028 if payments on account also fall due that day.

The January figure can be larger than the bill

Payments on account are two instalments towards your next bill, including Class 4. Each is usually half of the tax you owed last year. They are due by midnight on 31 January and 31 July. You do not have to make them if last year’s tax was less than £1,000, or if you paid more than 80% of that tax outside Self Assessment.

If 2026/27 is your first year, you usually pay the full bill plus the first instalment on 31 January 2028, not in January 2027. HMRC’s published Making Tax Digital timeline gives 31 January 2028 as the deadline to submit the 2026 to 2027 return. The payments on account guide walks through HMRC’s own £3,000 example.

On the £30,000 example, half of £4,531.80 is £2,265.90. If payments on account are required, 31 January 2028 is £6,797.70 and 31 July 2028 is £2,265.90. Dividing the January figure by 12 is £566.48 a month. That is also a planning aid, not a bill from HMRC.

What this leaves out

  • Student loan repayments, the High Income Child Benefit Charge, and Marriage Allowance
  • Savings, dividends, and capital gains
  • VAT, which uses taxable turnover rather than profit. See the £90,000 threshold guide

Questions

How much should a UK sole trader set aside for tax in 2026/27?

There is no single percentage. On £30,000 of taxable trading profit and no other income, in England, Wales or Northern Ireland, the estimate is £4,531.80 of Income Tax and Class 4. A first-year January 2028 payment can be £6,797.70 if payments on account apply. Use the calculator for a different profit. This is an estimate, not tax advice.

Does the 2026/27 set-aside include payments on account?

£4,531.80 is the estimated 2026/27 bill only. The instalments towards the next year are extra. In a first year they usually fall on 31 January 2028 and 31 July 2028.

Do these 2026/27 rates apply in Scotland?

No. Scotland has its own Income Tax bands. This page and the calculator use England, Wales and Northern Ireland rates only.
